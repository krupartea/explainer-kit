# Principles

A video is one story, told by one person, over one picture that keeps changing. These seven principles are all
the guidance there is. `toolbox.md` holds techniques to reach for when a principle is hard to meet in a specific
place; it is never a checklist, and a video that uses none of it can be perfect.

## 1. One insight

Before planning anything, write two lines at the top of narration.txt:

    // insight: the sentence the viewer should walk away with
    // click: the moment it should click, and what is on screen when it does

Everything in the video exists to set up that moment, let it happen, and let it land. A fact, a number or a
whole scene that doesn't serve it goes, however true or interesting. If you can't state the insight in one
sentence, you don't understand the topic well enough yet: read the sources again.

## 2. One picture that evolves

The video is one continuous canvas. New ideas grow out of objects already on screen: the thing the viewer has
been watching moves, splits, gets measured, turns into the next idea. A viewer who looked away for five seconds
should still recognize what they're looking at. When the story really moves to a new subject, the picture
transforms or clears as part of the story, never as a cut between slides.

No title cards, no text slides, no dashboard furniture (panels, step strips, recap chips, tables of results).
Text on screen labels the picture; the narration is never written on screen.

## 3. Let the viewer see it first

An aha can't be announced. It happens when the viewer notices something a moment before being told. So pose the
question, let the picture show the answer while the voice stays quiet or only points, then name what they saw.
The best moments are the ones the viewer could have predicted from what they'd already been shown. If a moment
works only because the narration says it is important, it isn't a moment yet: change the picture.

## 4. Need before name

The viewer should want the thing before it is named, and know what it is before how it works. How the need
arises depends on the topic: the obvious approach failing, a puzzle, a surprising number, a question the viewer
already has. Choose what is true to this topic; there is no fixed sequence.

## 5. Talk like a person

The narration is one person explaining something they find beautiful to a curious friend beside them, both
looking at the same picture. Sentences follow from each other ("so", "but", "which means"); their length varies;
the words are plain. Silence while the picture works is part of the narration.

Write the whole narration as speech first and hear it in your head from start to end. Add markup afterwards,
only where the voice would otherwise get the meaning wrong (narration-markup.md). Feeling comes from what is
said and shown, never from tags.

## 6. Few numbers, felt

Show a number when it makes something visible: this overlap is large, that one is tiny. Say only the numbers
the argument needs, and let the viewer feel their size against something on screen. Exact values go on screen;
the voice rounds. Numbers read aloud one after another are a ledger, not an explanation.

## 7. True

Every claim and every number traces to a source listed in brief.md: a file, a doc, a paper, or your own run of
the code. Unsure of a fact: say less. Names, numbers and identifiers exactly as in the source.

## Judging the whole

Judge the story, not the sentences. Before narrating, and once more after the final render, ask:

- Retell the idea in two sentences from the video alone. Is it the insight line?
- Where would a viewer lose the thread? Where does the picture jump instead of evolve?
- Is the click earned by what came before, or announced?
- What is there because it's true rather than because the story needs it?

Fix what these questions find by changing the story or the picture. Polishing single lines comes last.
